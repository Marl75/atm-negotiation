How it works

The AI negotiates; code checks the rules; a human decides

1 · CODE

Build the picture

From one late flight: arrival queue, runways, gates, crew hours, fuel, from the challenge workbook. No AI.

2 · AI

Negotiate

Each round, three separate Claude calls: Tower (ATC), Airline (Pilot), Ramp (Ground). Each sees only its own team’s data and answers in a fixed format: accept or reject, which rule, a counter-proposal.

3 · CODE

Check and choose

Fixed rules check every proposal. Code keeps the safest, then cheapest. Three rounds at most; then a solver decides and writes down why.

4 · HUMAN

Decide

The supervisor sees the plan, every check and each team’s concerns, then approves or rejects. Everything is logged.

3 to 9 AI calls per flight · a few cents · about 10 seconds · the 10,000-flight audit and all tests run without AI

The airline learns before take-off that it needs a fresh crew, not at the gate with passengers waiting.

≈9,000 flights in one year of challenge data had no legal plan at all (estimate).

Sometimes there is no good answer: here the crew would go past its 13-hour limit whatever the plan. The tool does not force anything. It tells the supervisor why, before take-off, so the airline can find a fresh crew. I estimate about nine thousand such flights in the data. (1:35–1:55) If asked how I know my results are honest: a DOT column filled in after the flight explained my “perfect” predictions (21% exact on those rows vs 3% on clean rows); I isolated it and only report accuracy on clean rows.

Rules of play: “any limit you change must be stated”

Where I depart from the challenge workbook

1 · Turnaround forecast per airline. Fit Jan–Jun, tested Jul–Dec; never below the workbook minimum, which stays the limit.

2 · FAA B757 wake category kept. All workbook wake pairs are identical, checked by a test.

3 · Airport noise curfews added. SNA and LGB, cited; not in the workbook.

4 · Fuel read conservatively. 45 min on arrival is total holding fuel: 15 min usable above the 30-min reserve.

5 · Half of runway ops are arrivals. Spacing = workbook ops/hour ÷ 2 per runway, or wake separation if larger.

6 · Ground shifts at 06:00, 14:00, 22:00. 8-hour length and 1.5× overtime from the workbook.

7 · Airline gates by traffic share. Airport gates × the airline’s share of arrivals there; spare gates as given.

Everything else comes from challenge2_operational_constraints.xlsx: runways, ops/hour, gates, turnaround, wake, fuel, crew, gate notice
